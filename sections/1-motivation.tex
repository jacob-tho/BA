Optimal transport has attracted increasing attention in mathematics and its applications in recent decades, although its origins date back to the eighteenth century. The classical problem concerns finding an optimal way of transporting mass between two given distributions while minimizing a prescribed transportation cost. Its dynamic formulation extends this perspective by describing the evolution of a density over time through a velocity field. This provides a mathematical framework for studying transport processes and motivates extensions that account for additional physical effects, such as diffusion, external forces, and the creation or removal of mass.

In this thesis, we investigate an inverse problem in the context of dynamical optimal transport. Rather than prescribing both the initial and final distributions, we consider density measurements along a given space-time curve and a prescribed velocity field. The objective is to reconstruct a density evolution consistent with these observations, including the unknown initial distribution. Since the available measurements generally do not uniquely determine the underlying transport process, additional regularization is required. To this end, we build upon the dynamical formulation introduced by Benamou and Brenier and extend it by incorporating diffusion, source and sink terms, and a penalty for deviations from the observed data.

More precisely, we consider the following optimization problem:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\min_{(\rho,J,f,g)}\quad
&\int_0^1\int_\Omega
\left(
\frac{|J_t|^2}{2\rho_t}
+\alpha_f^2\frac{|f_t|^2}{2\rho_t}
+\alpha_{\mathrm{dif}}^2\frac{|g_t|^2}{2\rho_t}
\right)\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dt\\
&\quad+\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{inv}}^2}{2}
\int_0^1\int_\Gamma|\rho-\rho_{\mathrm{data}}|^2\,\mathrm d x \mathrm d t,\\[4pt]
\text{s.t.}\quad
&\begin{cases}
\partial_t\rho_t+\nabla\cdot(J_t+\rho_t v_t^{\mathrm{wind}})=f_t
&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,1),\\
(J_t+\rho_t v_t^{\mathrm{wind}})\cdot n=0
&\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,1),\\
g_t=\nabla\rho_t
&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,1).
\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\]

Here, \(\rho_t\) denotes the time-dependent density, \(J_t\) the transport flux, \(f_t\) a source or sink term, and \(g_t\) an auxiliary variable representing the density gradient. The prescribed vector field \(v_t^{\mathrm{wind}}\) describes external transport, while \(\rho_{\mathrm{data}}\) represents the available measurements along the curve \(\Gamma\). The parameters \(\alpha_f\), \(\alpha_{\mathrm{dif}}\), and \(\alpha_{\mathrm{inv}}\) control the relative contributions of the source, diffusion, and data-fitting terms. The no-flux boundary condition ensures that mass cannot enter or leave the domain through its boundary, while changes in the total mass are permitted through the source term.

The thesis is structured as follows. Chapter 2 introduces the necessary mathematical foundations from measure theory, functional analysis, and variational calculus. Chapter 3 reviews classical optimal transport, beginning with Monge's formulation and Kantorovich's relaxation, before introducing the dynamical formulation and its relationship to the static problem. We then discuss the Benamou–Brenier approach and its numerical solution using an augmented Lagrangian algorithm (ALG2). Building on these results, Chapter 4 develops the extended inverse transport model and derives the corresponding numerical scheme. Finally, Chapter 5 presents and discusses numerical results obtained from its implementation.
